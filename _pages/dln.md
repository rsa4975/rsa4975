# gemini
As a 20-year-old university student double-majoring in Interactive Media and Literature, sitting in a classroom filled with peers from Computer Science, Environmental Studies, Engineering, and Film, I often find myself operating at the intersection of story and system. At this age, my generation is frequently labeled as "digital natives." The assumption is that because we grew up with smartphones in our hands, we possess an innate mastery over technology. However, my journey in higher education has revealed a stark difference between fluent digital consumption and genuine digital literacy. 

In my daily life, content creation and social interaction are second nature, but they rely on structured strategies. For creative projects, I move fluidly between toolsets: drafting visual assets and UI prototypes in Figma, editing video in Adobe Premiere, and leveraging Markdown platforms like Notion to manage project documentation and narrative outlines. Social interaction across digital spaces is similarly tactical; it requires navigating platform algorithms, maintaining distinct personal and academic identities, and evaluating source credibility in real-time. 

When it comes to research, data management, and analysis, my coursework has introduced me to platforms beyond standard search engines. For collecting and organizing literature, I rely on Zotero to maintain clean bibliographies and track metadata. For data manipulation, I bridge the qualitative and quantitative divide: I use Python (specifically Pandas and Jupyter Notebooks) to clean data sets and extract patterns, while utilizing tools like Visual Studio Code and GitHub to manage version control for dynamic web projects. 

Yet, walking into an interdisciplinary classroom highlights just how varied our technical toolkits really are. A Computer Science major views data through the lens of algorithmic efficiency and system architecture. An Environmental Studies student approaches data through spatial mapping, remote sensing, and ecological modeling. A Film and New Media student analyzes image frames, narrative structure, and sensory immersion. A Creative Writing major probes language for subtext, nuance, and structural rhythm. 

This is where the Digital Humanities offer transformative new horizons. DH isn't simply about applying tech tools to traditional humanities subjects; it is a critical framework that asks how digital mediums transform what it means to analyze culture, history, and human expression. For a Literature major, text is no longer just words on a static page—it becomes a dataset capable of being visualized through distant reading, topic modeling, and network analysis of character interactions. For an Interactive Media major, digital artifacts are not just code—they are cultural texts embedded with bias, historical context, and ethical implications.

Collaborating across these disciplines opens up essential avenues for developing "computational thinking"—the ability to break complex problems down into logic, patterns, and abstractions. When I pair with an Engineer or a CS major on a project, I learn how to structure data logically and build scalable systems. In return, my background in narrative and media critique helps ensure that the systems we build are human-centered, context-aware, and ethically grounded. 

This collaborative synergy becomes even more vital as we navigate the rapidly evolving world of artificial intelligence. Working with data in the age of generative AI requires us to move beyond viewing models as mere automated assistants. We must learn to interrogate the training sets behind large language models, analyze algorithmic bias, and understand how data curation impacts output. Learning to approach AI critically means recognizing it as both a technical tool and a cultural phenomenon that reshapes how knowledge is produced and consumed.

Reflecting on my current abilities, I recognize both my strengths and my blind spots. While I am adept at media production, qualitative critique, and front-end design, my quantitative data manipulation skills are still developing. In this course, I am discovering that code is not an intimidating black box, but rather a flexible medium for inquiry. I am realizing that computational tools do not replace critical reading or artistic intuition; instead, they amplify them, allowing us to ask questions at a scale previously impossible.

Looking ahead, the new ways of thinking I want to explore involve deep computational creativity and ethical data visualization. I want to learn how to build interactive narratives that respond dynamically to real-world data, transforming abstract metrics about issues like climate change or social migration into immersive human stories. By continuing to blur the lines between technology and the humanities, I hope to cultivate a digital literacy that is not just functional, but deeply critical, creative, and collaborative.
